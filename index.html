How Learn works

Rise through the ranks of Sumer. As a scribe you start with the signs that have the fewest wedges; master every sign in a rank to move up, from priest to king, and finally to the names of the gods. Each new sign you get right earns 10 points. You can look up any sign at any time on the Signs tab, whatever your rank.

Signs you’ve learned come back as refreshers, further apart each time you remember them, for 5 points each. Every few cards, look at a real tablet or meet someone who helped us read them again.

Phrasebook

Each phrase is marked as attested in real texts or built from attested parts. Tap any piece of a transliteration, such as ŋu₁₀, to see how to say it and what it means. Use “Copy cuneiform” to paste a phrase into a message.

How Sumerian sounded, as far as we know

Pronunciation is reconstructed, mostly from lists in which Akkadian-speaking scribes spelled out how Sumerian words were read. Vowels and most consonants are fairly secure. Stress, tone and a few sounds are still debated, so treat the “Say it” lines as a good approximation.

a e i uPure vowels, as in Spanish or Italian: ah, eh, ee, oo. There is no written o.
gAlways hard, as in go, never as in gem.
ŋThe ng in sing, even at the start of a syllable: ŋu₁₀ is ngoo.
šsh as in ship.
ḫLike the ch in Scottish loch or German Bach.
zUncertain. Probably closer to ts than English z, so this app says it as ts.
₂ ₃ ₁₀Not pronounced. The small numbers only tell you which sign was written.
{d}Markers like the god sign are silent. They were cues for the reader.

Signs and words

Grouped into the same ranks as Learn, from the scribe’s simplest signs to the names of the gods. Within each rank, signs go from fewest wedges to most. One sign often has several readings. The small numbers tell you which sign was written: du, du₃ and du₆ sound alike but are different signs.

Cuneiform through time

Cuneiform was used for more than three thousand years, and Sumerian outlived the Sumerians by nearly two thousand. Dates are approximate, and the names of periods are the ones museums and scholars use.

  1. ca. 3300–2900 BCE

    Uruk and Jemdet Nasr

    Writing begins in southern Mesopotamia as a way to keep accounts. Signs are drawn or pressed into clay, mostly numbers and goods.

    In this app: the proto-cuneiform barley account

  2. ca. 2900–2350 BCE

    Early Dynastic

    Sumerian city-states such as Ur, Uruk, Lagash and Nippur. The signs turn into wedges, and scribes begin writing royal inscriptions and the first literature.

    In this app: the tablet from Nippur

  3. ca. 2350–2150 BCE

    Akkadian Empire

    Sargon of Akkad unites the region. Akkadian, a Semitic language unrelated to Sumerian, becomes a language of government, written with the same signs. Sumerian is still spoken in the south.

    In this app: Enheduanna, Sargon’s daughter, who wrote hymns in Sumerian

  4. ca. 2150–2000 BCE

    Neo-Sumerian: Lagash II and Ur III

    After Akkad falls, Sumerian rulers take back control. Gudea of Lagash and the kings of the Third Dynasty of Ur write in Sumerian, and their state leaves behind tens of thousands of tablets. Museums often label objects from this time “Neo-Sumerian,” meaning the Sumerian revival after Akkad.

    In this app: Gudea, Ur-Namma’s foundation figure, the messengers’ rations

  5. ca. 2000–1600 BCE

    Old Babylonian

    Sumerian stops being spoken day to day, and Akkadian takes over. Sumerian lives on as a learned language for religion, law and literature, much as Latin did in medieval Europe. Students copy it in the eduba, and most Sumerian literature survives in their copies.

    In this app: the student’s practice tablet

  6. ca. 1600 BCE–80 CE

    Later Mesopotamia and beyond

    Cuneiform spreads across the Near East and is adapted for Hittite, Elamite, Urartian and other languages. Babylonian and Assyrian scribes keep copying Sumerian hymns and incantations, often with an Akkadian translation line by line. The last tablets that can be dated are astronomical records from the 1st century CE. After that, the script was forgotten until scholars deciphered it in the 1800s.

    In this app: the sign shapes, which follow the later Neo-Assyrian style used in modern sign lists

Tools this app uses

  • Cuneiform text is standard Unicode, encoded from a proposal by Michael Everson, Karljürgen Feuerherm and Steve Tinney (Unicode 5.0, 2006).
  • Signs are drawn with Noto Sans Cuneiform from Google’s Noto project, under the SIL Open Font License.
  • Meanings and readings follow the conventions of the electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary (ePSD2).
  • The voice was generated with ElevenLabs and checked by ear, sound by sound. Pronunciation follows the standard scholarly reconstruction described in the Phrasebook guide, with a few choices of our own where scholars disagree or the evidence runs out: z is said as ts, r is tapped or rolled, and stress is evenly spread because nobody knows where it fell.
